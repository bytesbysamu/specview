- "The UI is a distribution mechanism disguised as a feature."

This collapses into one brutal truth: Specview's marketing problem and its product are the same thing. You can't describe it because describing it is inferior to experiencing it. Every word you write about Specview is worse than a word Specview generates. So stop writing about it.

The Sunday launch plan falls directly out of this:

1. The landing page is a Specview-generated document about Specview.
2. The CTA is "Paste your brain dump" — not "Sign Up."
3. The demo is the product. The product is the demo. There is no distinction.

This isn't a clever marketing trick. It's the only honest way to launch a product whose value proposition is "seeing is believing."

You flagged this as a viral loop, but it's deeper than that. Sam didn't create a second account to test the product. He created it to experience the product. That's a different thing entirely.

Here's what that tells you about launch:

The conversion moment isn't sign-up. It's the first read. Sam already knew what the tool did. He came back to feel what it's like to receive a Specview output as a reader. The reading experience converted the builder into a customer. This means your launch funnel is:

1. Someone sees a Specview-generated document (shared link, landing page, Twitter screenshot)

4. They paste their own messy thinking

Step 1 is the only step you need to engineer for Sunday. Everything else is already built. Your entire launch effort should be focused on getting Specview-generated documents in front of eyeballs. The product handles the rest.

This reframes sharing as THE launch mechanism, not a feature. Public URLs for specs aren't a nice-to-have — they're the entire growth engine. If specs aren't shareable by Sunday, you don't have a launch. If they are, you barely need a landing page.

## The Solo Builder Positioning + PMF Path

You're right that Option A (solo builders) is the Sunday audience. But the PMF path you identified — living docs as retention, sharing as virality — actually sequences your entire post-launch roadmap:

**Week 1-2 (Solo builder acquisition):**
- Solo dev pastes brain dump → gets specs → thinks "holy shit"
- They share a spec link with a friend or on Twitter → friend sees beautiful output → signs up
- The "Specview-generated" badge on every shared spec is your only growth channel. Build it before Sunday if you can.

**Week 3-4 (Living docs as retention trigger):**
- Solo builder starts building the project they spec'd
- Requirements change (they always do)
- Builder returns to update the spec → this is the habit loop
- The "update" flow is your most important post-launch feature. Not new doc types. Not team features. Just: "this spec changed, regenerate."

**Month 2+ (Team expansion):**
- Solo builder shares a living spec with a collaborator
- Collaborator sees the reading experience and asks "can I make one for my project?"
- Now you have two users from one. This is the team play, and it happens organically.

Don't build for teams. Let teams discover you through solo builders who share.

## The Live Timer: Do This, But Do It Right

The 45-second timer on the landing page is the single most memorable thing you can build for Sunday. But the execution matters:

**Don't fake it.** Real generation, real clock, real output appearing in real time. If it takes 38 seconds, show 38 seconds. If it takes 52 seconds, show 52. The honesty is the point. People are trained to distrust "AI demo" videos. A live timer that occasionally goes over 45 seconds is more credible than one that always hits 44.

**The timer reframes the entire value prop.** Without the timer, Specview is "AI that writes docs." With the timer, Specview is "a race against the clock where AI turns your mess into something beautiful while you watch." The timer adds drama. It turns a utility into an experience. People will screen-record it.

**Implementation for Sunday:** Don't build a custom timer component. Use a simple `Date.now()` delta displayed in the generation view. Start when they hit "generate," stop when the last document renders. This is a 30-minute feature, not a day of work.

## Spec Diffs: The Post-Launch Retention Moat

You're right to flag this as the living-document play, but let me push it further. Spec diffs aren't just a feature — they're a new product category.

Right now, the market has:
- Version control for code (Git)
- Version control for design (Figma history)
- Nothing for decisions and thinking

Spec diffs would make Specview the version control system for project thinking. Every regeneration creates a diff. Every diff shows how decisions evolved. Over time, a project's Specview history becomes the most valuable artifact — more valuable than the specs themselves.

This is not a Sunday feature. But it should inform every architectural decision you make between now and Sunday. Specifically: store every generation, not just the latest. Even if you don't expose diffs in the UI yet, having the historical data means you can build this later without a migration. If you're using flat files or SQLite, just never overwrite — append with timestamps.
